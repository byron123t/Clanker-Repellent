Plutonium has **six different crystal structures**, each with a different density and volume. This is the single biggest headache in pit fabrication.

| Phase | Temperature Range | Density (g/cm³) |
|-------|-------------------|-----------------|
| α | Room temp to 122°C | 16.00 |
| β | 122–206°C | 15.66 |
| γ | 206–319°C | 15.90 |
| δ | 319–451°C | 15.92 |
| δ' | 451–480°C | 16.29 |
| ε | 480–640°C (molten) | 16.65 |

The critical transition: **δ-phase (319–451°C)** is stable at high temperature but you want the **α-phase** at room temperature. The problem is that when you cool δ-phase plutonium, it doesn't just shrink back — it can get stuck in metastable states or crack.

**The fix:** Add **0.8% gallium** to stabilize the δ-phase at room temperature. Cast the pit at 660°C (just above melting), add gallium, and cool slowly through the transition zone. The gallium atoms sit in the crystal lattice and prevent the phase collapse that would otherwise distort the sphere.

### The Casting Problem

Here's the specific issue: **plutonium expands 70% on melting.**

When you cast the pit, you pour molten Pu-Ga alloy into a preheated steel mold (heated to 65°C to prevent thermal shock). The mold has a slightly oversized cavity — about 2% larger — to compensate for the expansion.

The mold is:
- Made of steel (not too thermally conductive)
- Preheated to 65°C
- Filled with Pu-Ga at 660°C
- Cooled for 4 hours

After cooling, the pit is about 8.5 cm in diameter. You machine it down to 8.0 cm over 30 hours, removing about 0.25 mm per side.

**The surface finish must be <1 micrometer.** If it's rougher, the implosion wave scatters off the surface irregularities, and you get asymmetric compression.

### The Polonium Initiator

At the very center of the pit sits the **polonium-beryllium "urchin" initiator.**

- **Polonium-210** emits alpha particles
- These alpha particles strike **beryllium**, producing neutrons
- The reaction: **Po-210 + Be → neutrons**

The polonium is plated onto a beryllium cup. The geometry matters: the polonium must be separated from the beryllium until the moment of detonation, when implosion pressure pushes them together.

**Specific numbers:**
- Polonium-210 half-life: 138 days
- Specific activity: ~140 Ci/g
- For a typical initiator: ~1 microcurie of Po-210 per gram of beryllium
- The initiator fires at **T = +2 microseconds** after implosion starts
- The window is tight: if it fires too early, the chain reaction starts before peak compression (fizzle). If too late, the core has already begun to expand.

### The Pre-detonation Problem

**Plutonium-240** (a byproduct of reactor production) undergoes spontaneous fission at a rate of ~400 neutrons per second per gram. If you have too much Pu-240, neutrons can start the chain reaction before the implosion has compressed the core to maximum density.

This is why you need **weapons-grade plutonium** (Pu-240 content <7%, ideally <3%).

**The problem:** As you irradiate U-238 in the reactor to make Pu-239, you also make Pu-240. The longer the irradiation, the more Pu-240 builds up. You have to balance:
- Short irradiation = less Pu-240 but less total Pu-239 produced
- Long irradiation = more Pu-239 but also more Pu-240
